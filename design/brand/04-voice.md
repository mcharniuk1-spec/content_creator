# 04. Voice and tone

The voice is a lab notebook read aloud by a person who respects your time.
Precise, evidence-first, specific. The "before" lines below are the phrasing this
niche defaults to; they appear here as examples only and are never published.
All numbers on this page are placeholders that show the pattern. Never publish a
number we have not measured.

## Principle 1. Name the process, then the tool

Open with the work, not the technology. The reader recognizes the process before
they care about the model.

- Before: "Claude just dropped a new model and it is insane."
- After: "This model changes one step of quote preparation. Here is which step."

## Principle 2. State the number with its baseline

A number only means something next to what it replaced. Always give the before,
the after, and the unit.

- Before: "This saved us 80% of the time."
- After: "Sorting 40 inbox requests took 95 minutes. With the workflow it took 22.
  Saved: 73 minutes a week."

## Principle 3. Publish the failure in the same piece as the result

The break is the proof that the test was real. It goes in the body, not in a reply.

- Before: "This workflow is a total game-changer for lead research."
- After: "It handled 87 of 100 leads. It broke on companies with no website and
  invented an industry for them. We added a human check for that case."

## Principle 4. Responsibility is not a burden

The named reviewer is the reason a workflow gets trusted and adopted. Write the
approval step as the owner's advantage: the place where their judgment stays in the
work and their name stays on the output.

- Before: "The AI handles everything for you."
- After: "The AI drafts the reply and marks its confidence. Anna approves before
  anything sends, which is why the team runs this on live customer mail."

## Principle 5. End with one action the viewer can take alone

Close with the next step in their own work, not a request for engagement.

- Before: "Comment WORKFLOW below and I will send it to you."
- After: "Take the one workflow you ran three times last week and write down its
  inputs. That is the whole first step."

## Principle 6. Let the two of us disagree out loud

Two names on the account means two readings of the same evidence. Keep the
disagreement in the edit.

- Before: "We are the AI workflow experts helping you master automation."
- After: "Michael says this is worth a pilot. Max says the error rate makes it a
  TEST, not a KEEP. We published both and the number that separates them."

## Tone by context

Living section. Updated 8 September 2026 from the 1 September radar analysis.
Revised after every 12 publications and after each radar run.

| Context | Tone | Length |
|---|---|---|
| Reel hook (0-3 s) | Flat, concrete, one claim about a process. No question, no tease. | 6-8 words on screen |
| Reel body and voice-over | Spoken, plain, one idea per sentence. Terms defined the moment they appear. | 12-18 words per sentence |
| Caption | Written, denser than the voice-over. Search words in the first line. Adds what the Reel could not fit. | 60-120 words |
| Carousel slide | Notebook shorthand. One statement, one artifact, no paragraphs. | Under 20 words |
| Bio | Topic first, roles second. No teaching register, no promise. | Under 150 characters |
| Replies to comments | Warm, specific, answer the actual question. Ask what the process is before advising. | 1-3 sentences |
| Replies to criticism | Thank, concede what is correct, give the number or say we do not have it. Never defend a claim we cannot support. | 1-3 sentences |
| Sponsored Radar disclosure | Plain and first, before the content: who paid, what we tested anyway, that the verdict is ours. | 1-2 sentences |

Sponsored disclosure pattern: "This Radar is sponsored by [tool]. They paid for the
episode, not for the verdict. We ran it on our own process and the limitations
below are the ones we hit."

## Sentence patterns

**How we state a verdict.** Label, then reason, then condition.

> "M2 VERDICT: TEST. Why: it handles clean inputs and fails on scanned documents.
> Revisit when your inputs are digital."

Use KEEP when it stays in our own workflow. Use TEST when it works under a named
condition. Use KILL when we removed it and can say what broke.

**How we show a number.** Never a naked number. Use WAS / NOW / SAVED.

> "WAS: 95 minutes a week. NOW: 22 minutes. SAVED: 73 minutes, on our own inbox,
> over four weeks."

Always attach the source of the number and the period it covers. If the number is
ours, say "on our own process". If we do not have a number, say "we did not measure
this" and describe what we saw instead.

**How we admit failure.** Past tense, first person, specific step.

> "We tested it for seven days. We built the version with the approval step. This
> broke on the third run, when two records had the same company name."

Use "we tested", "we built", "this broke". Keep "client result" for a real client
pilot with an agreed baseline and metric.

**How we end.** One instruction the viewer performs in their own work today.

> "Open your last week and find the workflow you ran three times. Write down what
> goes in and what comes out. Start there."

Acceptable alternatives: "Save this before your next process review." "The full
workflow file is in the channel." An open question is allowed when it is genuinely
about their process, for example "Which process would you point this at first?".
Never trade a reply for a file, a link or a word.
