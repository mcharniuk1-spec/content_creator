# M2 Lab Psychology Evidence Initial Research

**Question:** What can psychology research contribute to choosing and staying committed to a positioning hypothesis?

## Direct answer

Psychology cannot identify the commercially best audience or positioning. Customer interviews, founder capability, and market evidence do that. Psychology can improve the decision process: limit unfocused option-search, make commitment explicit, pre-agree review rules, and translate a chosen experiment into actions.

## Evidence that is usable for M2 Lab

### 1. Too many options are not automatically the problem

Chernev, Böckenholt, and Goodman’s meta-analysis of 99 observations (N = 7,202) found that choice overload is conditional, not a universal rule. Greater choice-set complexity, difficult decisions, uncertain preferences, and an effort-minimizing goal made overload more likely. The practical use is to work from a short, fixed set of hypotheses and explicit criteria; do not explain every instance of indecision as “our brains have too many options.”

Source: [Choice overload: A conceptual review and meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002), *Journal of Consumer Psychology*, 2015.

Important limitation: this is consumer-choice research, not a study of two founders choosing a market position. Another meta-analysis found high heterogeneity and no reliable universal choice-overload effect, so the claim must remain conditional.

Source: [Can There Ever Be Too Many Options? A Meta-Analytic Review of Choice Overload](https://doi.org/10.1086/651235), *Journal of Consumer Research*, 2010.

### 2. A chosen focus needs an if-then protocol

Gollwitzer and Sheeran’s meta-analysis of 94 independent tests found that implementation intentions - plans in the form “if situation X happens, then I do Y” - had a medium-to-large effect on goal attainment (d = .65). For M2 Lab, this supports rules such as: “If a new niche idea appears, add it to the parking lot; discuss it only in the Friday review.” It does not establish that the original positioning choice is commercially correct.

Source: [Implementation Intentions and Goal Achievement: A Meta-analysis of Effects and Processes](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1), *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology*, 2006.

### 3. Commitment makes alternative directions less mentally dominant

Shah, Friedman, and Kruglanski ran six studies on goal shielding. They found that activating a focal goal can inhibit the accessibility of alternative goals; the result was moderated by commitment to the focal goal. For M2 Lab, the right use is a clear, time-bounded experiment plus a parking lot for alternatives - not suppressing useful market evidence or making an irreversible decision.

Source: [Forgetting All Else: On the Antecedents and Consequences of Goal Shielding](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.83.6.1261), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 2002.

### 4. A verbal yes is not enough commitment

Klein, Wesson, Hollenbeck, and Alge synthesised 83 independent samples on goal commitment. The practical implication is to ask each founder separately whether they will honor the test, what would justify exit, and what obstacle could make them abandon it. A common sentence in a meeting is weaker evidence than two explicit commitments.

Source: [Goal Commitment and the Goal-Setting Process: Conceptual Clarification and Empirical Synthesis](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-9010.84.6.885), *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 1999.

### 5. A shared deadline makes self-control more concrete

Ariely and Wertenbroch’s experiments on precommitment found that self-imposed deadlines improved task performance, although externally imposed deadlines could be more effective. For M2 Lab, the support is for a shared eight-week window, weekly review, and an external scorecard. The founders must voluntarily adopt this rule; it should not become a punishment mechanism.

Source: [Procrastination, Deadlines, and Performance: Self-Control by Precommitment](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00441), *Psychological Science*, 2002.

### 6. Contrast the desired result with the obstacle before committing

Wang, Wang, and Gai’s meta-analysis of mental contrasting with implementation intentions included 21 studies, 24 independent effect sizes, and 15,907 participants. It reported g = .336 and flagged publication-bias risk. The appropriate use is: before choosing, write the desired outcome, the most probable internal obstacle, and an if-then response. It is not evidence that positive visualization or a scorecard will create market demand.

Source: [A Meta-Analysis of the Effects of Mental Contrasting With Implementation Intentions on Goal Attainment](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.565202), *Frontiers in Psychology*, 2021.

## How the evidence changes the positioning workflow

1. Limit the candidates to three person-job hypotheses.
2. Choose using direct audience evidence and a written scoring rubric, not intuition alone.
3. Before starting, record each founder’s commitment, expected obstacle, and if-then response.
4. Run one time-bounded experiment with a shared review scorecard.
5. Park competing ideas until the scheduled review, unless pre-agreed disconfirming evidence appears.

## What this research does not answer

- Which person has the strongest pain or willingness to pay.
- Which service/product should be built first.
- Which Instagram post will perform best.
- Exact success thresholds for the M2 Lab 60-day test.

These require direct audience interviews, the existing Content Engine, and your own experiment data.
