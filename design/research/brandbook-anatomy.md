# Anatomy of a brand book (2025–2026) — research for M2 Lab

Method: primary brand-guideline sites/PDFs of real companies where reachable, plus
Nielsen Norman Group (NN/g) and Frontify as secondary design references. Every claim
below is tagged with the URL it came from. Where a source could not be read in full
(binary PDF, paywall), that is stated instead of guessing at its contents.

---

## 1. Canonical sections of a brand book

| Section | What it contains | Why it matters | Real brand books that include it |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Brand purpose / mission** | Why the brand exists, beyond making money | Anchors every later decision so rules have "strategic reason," not just taste | Uber's guidelines open with the mission statement "We ignite opportunity by setting the world in motion" ([Uber Brand guidelines summary](https://brandingstyleguides.com/guide/uber-2018/)); Herman Miller's brand site leads with vision/history ([Frontify examples](https://www.frontify.com/en/guide/brand-guidelines-examples)); Klarna's brand site opens on mission before visuals ([brand.klarna.com](https://brand.klarna.com/), via [Frontify examples](https://www.frontify.com/en/guide/brand-guidelines-examples)) |
| **Positioning** | Where the brand sits vs. alternatives, the category it competes in | Prevents messaging drift and copy-cat visuals | Frontify names positioning as part of the "brand core" that should precede logo/color work ([Frontify: Brand Guidelines](https://www.frontify.com/en/guide/brand-guidelines)) |
| **Audience** | Who the brand is for, in enough detail to write for them | Voice and content choices are audience-relative, not absolute | Same "brand core" bundle (mission, vision, values, positioning, **audience**, personality, promise) per Frontify ([Frontify: Brand Guidelines](https://www.frontify.com/en/guide/brand-guidelines)) |
| **Personality / values** | Adjectives and behaviours that describe the brand as if it were a person | Gives writers and designers a consistent filter for "on-brand" | Atlassian's FY2025 guidelines list "brand basics, principles, **personality**, logos, visual storytelling" ([getint.io summary of Atlassian guide](https://www.getint.io/blog/atlassian-terminology-logos)); Duolingo's brand guide frames Duo the owl as "funny, dramatic, sarcastic" to carry personality ([Canny Creative: Duolingo brand breakdown](https://www.canny-creative.com/brand-breakdown/brand/duolingo/)) |
| **Voice & tone (with do/don't pattern)** | Fixed voice (who we always are) vs. tone (how it flexes by context/emotion), often shown as do/don't or before/after copy pairs | Keeps writing recognisable across many authors while letting empathy adjust to the reader's situation | Mailchimp: "You have the same voice all the time, but your tone changes" across 4 principles (plainspoken, genuine, translators, dry humor) ([styleguide.mailchimp.com/voice-and-tone](https://styleguide.mailchimp.com/voice-and-tone/), [GitHub source](https://github.com/mailchimp/content-style-guide/blob/master/02-voice-and-tone.html.md)); NN/g frames tone of voice across 4 sliders — serious↔witty, respectful↔naughty, formal↔casual, objective↔enthusiastic ([Centigrade summary of NN/g voice & tone model](https://www.centigrade.de/en/blog/das-branding-tool-fur-ux-writers-der-voice-tone-guide/)); Dropbox has a dedicated "Voice & Tone" nav section, positioned right after "Framework" and before any visual section ([brand.dropbox.com](https://brand.dropbox.com/)) |
| **Messaging hierarchy (tagline, supporting lines)** | Primary line, secondary proof lines, how they nest in a layout | Stops every asset re-inventing the headline | Covered under "brand core / core promise" in Frontify's framework ([Frontify: Brand Guidelines](https://www.frontify.com/en/guide/brand-guidelines)) |
| **Naming rules** | How to write the brand's own name (capitalisation, trademark symbol, forbidden variants) | Legal protection + visual consistency in running text | Instagram's brand page requires "Instagram" to stay capitalized "in the same font size and style as the content surrounding it," plus trademark-protection rules ([Meta Brand Resource Center: Instagram](https://www.meta.com/brand/resources/instagram/instagram-brand/)); Mailchimp has a dedicated "Writing about Mailchimp" nav section ([styleguide.mailchimp.com](https://styleguide.mailchimp.com/)) |
| **Logo system (versions, clear space, min size, misuse)** | Primary/secondary lockups, icon-only mark, exclusion zone, smallest legible size, forbidden distortions | Protects legibility and legal trademark claims across every output size | Slack: "clear space around it should always be greater than or equal to the size of the octothorpe," a small-size variant below 30px, and explicit no-crop/no-rotate/no-distort rules ([Slack Media Kit](https://slack.com/media-kit), [Slack Brand Guidelines PDF](https://a.slack-edge.com/0f43e/marketing/img/media-kit/Slack-Brand-Guidelines.pdf)); Spotify: logo never smaller than 70px digital / 20mm print, icon never smaller than 21px / 6mm ([Designers Choice summary of Spotify guidelines](https://designers-choice.net/spotify-brand-guidelines/)); Atlassian: minimum 24px height digital / 0.5in print ([getint.io](https://www.getint.io/blog/atlassian-terminology-logos)) |
| **Color (roles, ratios, accessibility)** | Primary/secondary/neutral palettes with hex/RGB/CMYK/Pantone, usage ratios, accessible pairings | Keeps recognition consistent and meets contrast requirements | NN/g lists "primary and secondary [color palettes], with accessible combinations" as a core style-guide element ([NN/g: Design Systems vs. Style Guides](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/design-systems-vs-style-guides/)); Spotify defines Spotify Green as primary plus a neutral black/white/grey set, restricting the green logo to non-duotoned photography ([Designers Choice](https://designers-choice.net/spotify-brand-guidelines/)) |
| **Typography (families, roles, scale)** | Primary/secondary typefaces, weight and size scale, fallback web fonts, licensing notes | Consistency of "voice made visible" across print/digital/code | Spotify uses "Circular Std" as its defining typeface ([Designers Choice](https://designers-choice.net/spotify-brand-guidelines/)); Atlassian commissioned a custom geometric sans with rounded terminals to echo the mountain mark ([designyourway.net: Atlassian logo history](https://www.designyourway.net/blog/atlassian-logo/)); Dropbox has a standalone "Typography" nav section ([brand.dropbox.com](https://brand.dropbox.com/)) |
| **Iconography** | Icon grid, stroke weight, corner radius, allowed/forbidden icon styles | Small-scale visual consistency (favicons, UI, social icons) | Dropbox: dedicated "Iconography" section between Typography and Color ([brand.dropbox.com](https://brand.dropbox.com/)) |
| **Imagery / photography / video direction** | Subject matter, color grading, composition, do/don't photo examples, video look | Stops stock-photo mismatch and keeps a recognisable "feel" | NN/g lists "imagery (photography, illustration, and video styling guidance)" as a brand style-guide component ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/design-systems-vs-style-guides/)); Dropbox has an "Imagery" nav section ([brand.dropbox.com](https://brand.dropbox.com/)) |
| **Motion** | Easing, duration, when motion is/isn't used, signature transitions | Increasingly standard as brands live on video/social first | Dropbox's brand site ends its system with a "Motion" section ([brand.dropbox.com](https://brand.dropbox.com/)); Duolingo's guide explicitly covers "illustration style, motion, tone of voice" ([Canny Creative](https://www.canny-creative.com/brand-breakdown/brand/duolingo/)) |
| **Layout / grid** | Margins, columns, spacing units, composition rules | Gives templates a repeatable skeleton | Implied inside Frontify's "templates and applications" bucket ([Frontify: Brand Guidelines](https://www.frontify.com/en/guide/brand-guidelines)); Shopify's Polaris design language documents a 4px spacing rhythm and grid base ([Codersy: Shopify Polaris color guidelines](https://www.codersy.com/blog/shopify-ui-ux-and-design/top-7-shopify-polaris-color-guidelines-explained)) |
| **Social-media specifics** | Platform-by-platform formats, caption style, hashtag policy, response/DM tone | Increasingly the *primary* surface, not an appendix | Frontify's recommended list includes a "Social media standards" section (platform formats, caption style, hashtags, response rules) ([Frontify: Brand Guidelines](https://www.frontify.com/en/guide/brand-guidelines)); general social style-guide practice: define 3–5 voice traits, then layer platform-specific rules on top ([Hootsuite: Social media style guide](https://blog.hootsuite.com/social-media-style-guide/), [Sprinklr: Social media style guide](https://www.sprinklr.com/blog/social-media-style-guide/)) |
| **Examples of application** | Real mockups: a social post, a deck slide, an email, a merch item | Shows the system solving real layout problems instead of staying abstract | Herman Miller's brand site includes "downloadable templates" and case studies ([Frontify examples list](https://www.frontify.com/en/guide/brand-guidelines-examples)); Airbus's brand site offers "editable templates" ([same list](https://www.frontify.com/en/guide/brand-guidelines-examples)) |
| **Governance (who approves, how to request assets)** | Ownership, approval chain, exception process, update cadence, contact for non-standard use | Without this, a brand book is a PDF nobody is accountable to | Slack's guide has a named "Governance" section and directs off-guideline requests to feedback@slack.com with "a visual mockup of intended use" ([Slack Media Kit](https://slack.com/media-kit)); Instagram/Meta requires written permission and a two-step approval process for broadcast/media use ([Meta Brand Resource Center: Instagram](https://www.meta.com/brand/resources/instagram/instagram-brand/)); general governance practice: name an owner, an approval workflow, and an update-review cadence ([Marq: Brand governance framework](https://www.marq.com/blog/brand-governance/), [MangoApps: Brand Asset Approval SOP](https://www.mangoapps.com/templates/sop/brand-asset-approval-sop)) |

Note on sources I could not fully read: the Uber PDF (`assets.uber.com/d/k4nuxdZ8MC7E/user-guide`) and the Slack Brand Guidelines PDF returned raw, non-OCR'd binary content to the fetch tool, so their internal table of contents could not be verified directly — only what is confirmed via secondary summaries above (brandingstyleguides.com, getint.io) is cited. The Atlassian FY2025 brand book on Scribd was not opened (login-gated document viewer) — its structure above is cited only via the getint.io secondary summary.

## 2. Content / media brand (Instagram-first) vs. product / corporate brand

- **One channel dominates, so the guide can be thinner but sharper.** General social-style-guide practice is to define a shared voice once, then add only channel-specific overlays (caption length, emoji use) rather than building separate systems per platform — for a single-platform media brand, most of that overlay work collapses into "how we do Instagram." ([Hootsuite: Social media style guide](https://blog.hootsuite.com/social-media-style-guide/))
- **Editorial integrity rules replace legal trademark rules as the top governance concern.** A corporate brand book's governance section is about logo misuse and legal risk; a media brand's is about editorial standards — NPR's guidelines/style-guide use AP style as a base and exist to keep "a level of editorial consistency and excellence," not to police logo placement ([NPR Style Guide](https://www.npr.org/sections/npr-training/2025/06/01/g-s1-67492/npr-style-guide)); the BBC's Editorial Guidelines govern "independence, impartiality and editorial integrity" separately from its visual design system (GEL) ([Adele/UXPin: BBC GEL](https://adele.uxpin.com/bbc-gel)).
- **Content pillars are a brand-book element unique to media/social brands.** Product brand books rarely define recurring content themes; social/media guides typically fix 3–5 content pillars (e.g. education, behind-the-scenes, community) as a documented part of the system. ([Sprout Social: content pillars](https://sproutsocial.com/insights/social-media-content-pillars/), [Bazaarvoice: content pillars guide](https://www.bazaarvoice.com/blog/a-guide-to-content-pillars-for-social-media-and-beyond/))
- **The "logo system" shrinks; the "profile system" grows.** A media account run mostly on Instagram needs a profile photo, bio formula, highlight-cover system and grid rhythm documented in place of a multi-lockup logo suite — practitioner guides treat the Instagram grid as "a visual system + a content system + a conversion system" rather than a logo-usage manual. ([Slate: Branding Instagram](https://slateteams.com/blog/branding-instagram-guide), [Magnt: Instagram Branding Guide](https://magnt.com/instagram-branding-guide))
- **Voice is written by (or for) one or two people, not a large multi-team org.** Corporate brand guidelines exist because many disparate teams must sound the same; a small media brand's "voice" section functions closer to a personal-brand style sheet — personal brands "revolve around an individual['s] values, skills, and personality," while corporate brands "represent a company's mission, values, and products" for a wide, standardized audience ([Ramotion: Personal Brand vs. Business Brand](https://www.ramotion.com/blog/personal-brand-vs-business-brand/)).
- **Motion and video direction matter earlier.** Because Reels/video are often the primary format, a media brand needs its motion and video-look guidance up front, not as an appendix — this mirrors how product brands with heavy video use (Dropbox, Duolingo) give motion/illustration its own top-level section rather than folding it into "imagery." ([brand.dropbox.com](https://brand.dropbox.com/), [Canny Creative: Duolingo](https://www.canny-creative.com/brand-breakdown/brand/duolingo/))
- **Governance is lighter-weight by necessity.** Corporate governance sections assume a brand team fielding requests from agencies and partners (see Slack's feedback@slack.com escalation path); a 1–2 person media brand instead needs a simple internal checklist (who writes captions, who approves collabs) rather than a formal approval council. ([Slack Media Kit](https://slack.com/media-kit), general governance practice via [Marq](https://www.marq.com/blog/brand-governance/))
- **Positioning is against other creators/formats, not other SKUs.** A product brand's "positioning" section maps competing products; a content brand's maps competing accounts, formats and attention, which changes what the "audience" and "personality" sections need to justify.

## 3. Typical order of a brand book — 3 real examples

1. **Dropbox** (brand.dropbox.com): **Framework → Voice & Tone → Logo → Typography → Iconography → Color → Imagery → Motion.** Strategy and voice are placed before any visual-identity section. ([brand.dropbox.com](https://brand.dropbox.com/))
2. **Mailchimp Content Style Guide** (styleguide.mailchimp.com — a voice/content-only guide, no visual identity): **Writing Goals and Principles → Voice and Tone → Writing about Mailchimp → Writing About People → Composition rules and Mechanics → Web Elements → [channel-specific chapters: educational, legal, email, social, accessibility, translation] → Structured Content → Copyright/Trademarks → Word List.** Principles and voice come first; channel-specific writing rules (including social media) come after the foundational voice chapter. ([styleguide.mailchimp.com](https://styleguide.mailchimp.com/))
3. **Slack Brand Guidelines**: three top-level parts — **"Defining our brand" → "Design elements" (logo, color, typography, illustration) → "Governance."** Strategy/definition first, visual system second, rules-of-use last. ([Slack Media Kit](https://slack.com/media-kit) confirms this three-part structure)

General guidance consistent with all three real examples: **do not start with the logo.** Frontify's own guide states this explicitly — "Start with the brand strategy," because it gives "every rule strategic reason" — recommending Brand core (mission/vision/values/positioning/audience/personality/promise) as section one, with logo/color/type coming after. ([Frontify: Brand Guidelines](https://www.frontify.com/en/guide/brand-guidelines))

## 4. Common mistakes in brand books

- **Too rigid, so people work around it instead of using it.** "When designers feel boxed in by too many rules, they either ignore the guidelines entirely or produce work that looks lifeless" — over-specification is presented as a trust problem, not a documentation problem. ([ATNY Creative: Why Brand Guidelines Fail When They Become Too Rigid](https://anthonysotelo.com/why-brand-guidelines-fail-when-they-become-too-rigid/))
- **Going stale after a redesign or rebrand.** "Referencing a logo redesigned 18 months ago... trains people to ignore the whole system"; guidelines untouched for three years are described as functionally fiction. ([Maloo: Why Brand Guides Fail on Day One](https://www.maloo.ai/briefing/why-brand-guides-fail/))
- **Too long to be used under deadline.** A "60-slide PDF" that nobody reads cover-to-cover, and that nobody searches when they need one quick answer, is called out as a structural failure, not a content failure. ([BasemonKeys: 5 Common Mistakes to Avoid When Creating Your Brand Guidelines](https://basemonkeys.com/insights/5-common-mistakes-to-avoid-when-creating-your-brand-guidelines))
- **Treated as optional / non-binding.** A named misconception is that "brand guidelines are not mandatory or binding and can be ignored or modified at will" — without stated governance/ownership, this becomes self-fulfilling. ([LinkedIn Advice: Common misconceptions about brand guidelines](https://www.linkedin.com/advice/1/what-most-common-misconceptions-brand-guidelines))
- **Rules with no rationale.** Guidance that says only "don't do X" without the underlying strategic reason leaves people guessing and inviting exceptions; guidelines that instead frame the positive space ("here's what you can do") are reported as better followed. ([ATNY Creative](https://anthonysotelo.com/why-brand-guidelines-fail-when-they-become-too-rigid/), [Frontify: Brand Guidelines](https://www.frontify.com/en/guide/brand-guidelines) — "explaining the decisions behind the brand" is listed as what separates useful guidelines from basic ones)
- **Starting with visuals instead of strategy.** Frontify explicitly flags this as a mistake: skipping mission/positioning/audience and starting at the logo produces rules with no "strategic reason." ([Frontify: Brand Guidelines](https://www.frontify.com/en/guide/brand-guidelines))

## 5. Recommended table of contents — M2 Lab brand book (identity + voice + positioning)

Visual system (logo, color, type, grid) is intentionally out of scope here — it lives in a separate design-system document, per the working split for this project.

| # | Section | Must / Nice |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Purpose / mission — why M2 Lab exists, in one sentence | Must |
| 2 | Positioning — practical AI adoption vs. hype/news accounts; what M2 Lab is not | Must |
| 3 | Audience — who follows, what they already know, what they came for | Must |
| 4 | Personality / values — 3–5 traits, each with one line of explanation | Must |
| 5 | Voice — fixed traits, with do/don't caption examples | Must |
| 6 | Tone — how voice flexes by content type (tutorial vs. hot take vs. reply to a comment) | Must |
| 7 | Messaging hierarchy — tagline/bio line, 2–3 supporting lines for different contexts (bio, link-in-bio, pitch) | Must |
| 8 | Naming rules — how "M2 Lab" is written, capitalized, and referenced by others | Nice |
| 9 | Content pillars — the 3–5 recurring themes and what each is for | Must |
| 10 | Content mistakes / anti-examples — 2–3 concrete "not us" posts, so voice drift is easy to spot | Nice |
| 11 | Governance — who writes/approves captions and collabs, how a partner (Max) requests something off-template | Must |
| 12 | Review cadence — when this document gets re-checked against reality | Nice |

Rationale for the order: strategy (mission → positioning → audience → personality) before writing rules (voice → tone → messaging), per the pattern confirmed in Dropbox, Mailchimp and Slack's real guides and stated explicitly by Frontify (Section 3 above). Content pillars are inserted before governance because, per Section 2, they are the media-brand-specific element that most product-brand books skip — and governance is kept last and short, matching the "1–2 person media brand" pattern rather than a corporate approval council.
